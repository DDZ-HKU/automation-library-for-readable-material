# Identity Mappings in Deep Residual Networks

Kaiming He, Xiangyu Zhang, Shaoqing Ren, and Jian Sun

Microsoft Research

Abstract Deep residual networks [\[1\]](#page-14-0) have emerged as a family of extremely deep architectures showing compelling accuracy and nice convergence behaviors. In this paper, we analyze the propagation formulations behind the residual building blocks, which suggest that the forward and backward signals can be directly propagated from one block to any other block, when using identity mappings as the skip connections and after-addition activation. A series of ablation experiments support the importance of these identity mappings. This motivates us to propose a new residual unit, which makes training easier and improves generalization. We report improved results using a 1001-layer ResNet on CIFAR-10 (4.62% error) and CIFAR-100, and a 200-layer ResNet on ImageNet. # 1 Introduction

Deep residual networks (ResNets) [\[1\]](#page-14-0) consist of many stacked "Residual Units". Each unit (Fig. [1](#page-1-0) (a)) can be expressed in a general form:

$$\mathbf{y}_l = h(\mathbf{x}_l) + \mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}_l, \mathcal{W}_l),$$
  
 $\mathbf{x}_{l+1} = f(\mathbf{y}_l),$ 

where x<sup>l</sup> and xl+1 are input and output of the l-th unit, and F is a residual function. In [\[1\]](#page-14-0), h(xl) = x<sup>l</sup> is an identity mapping and f is a ReLU [\[2\]](#page-14-1) function.

ResNets that are over 100-layer deep have shown state-of-the-art accuracy for several challenging recognition tasks on ImageNet [\[3\]](#page-14-2) and MS COCO [\[4\]](#page-14-3) competitions. The central idea of ResNets is to learn the additive residual function F with respect to h(xl), with a key choice of using an identity mapping h(xl) = x<sup>l</sup> . This is realized by attaching an identity skip connection ("shortcut").

In this paper, we analyze deep residual networks by focusing on creating a "direct" path for propagating information — not only within a residual unit, but through the entire network. Our derivations reveal that if both h(xl) and f(yl) are identity mappings, the signal could be directly propagated from one unit to any other units, in both forward and backward passes. Our experiments empirically show that training in general becomes easier when the architecture is closer to the above two conditions.

These experiments suggest that keeping a "clean" information path (indicated by the grey arrows in Fig. [1,](#page-1-0) [2,](#page-4-0) and [4\)](#page-7-0) is helpful for easing optimization.

To construct an identity mapping f(yl) = y<sup>l</sup> , we view the activation functions (ReLU and BN [\[8\]](#page-14-7)) as "pre-activation" of the weight layers, in contrast to conventional wisdom of "post-activation". Based on this unit, we present competitive results on CIFAR-10/100 with a 1001-layer ResNet, which is much easier to train and generalizes better than the original ResNet in [\[1\]](#page-14-0). # 2 Analysis of Deep Residual Networks

The ResNets developed in [\[1\]](#page-14-0) are modularized architectures that stack building blocks of the same connecting shape. In this paper we call these blocks "Residual Units". The original Residual Unit in [\[1\]](#page-14-0) performs the following computation:

$$\mathbf{y}_l = h(\mathbf{x}_l) + \mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}_l, \mathcal{W}_l), \tag{1}$$

<span id="page-2-4"></span><span id="page-2-3"></span>
$$\mathbf{x}_{l+1} = f(\mathbf{y}_l). \tag{2}$$

Here x<sup>l</sup> is the input feature to the l-th Residual Unit. W<sup>l</sup> = {Wl,k|1≤k≤K} is a set of weights (and biases) associated with the l-th Residual Unit, and K is the number of layers in a Residual Unit (K is 2 or 3 in [\[1\]](#page-14-0)). F denotes the residual function, e.g., a stack of two 3×3 convolutional layers in [\[1\]](#page-14-0). The function f is the operation after element-wise addition, and in [\[1\]](#page-14-0) f is ReLU. The function h is set as an identity mapping: h(xl) = x<sup>l</sup> . [1](#page-2-2)

If f is also an identity mapping: xl+1 ≡ y<sup>l</sup> , we can put Eqn.[\(2\)](#page-2-3) into Eqn.[\(1\)](#page-2-4) and obtain:

<span id="page-2-5"></span>
$$\mathbf{x}_{l+1} = \mathbf{x}_l + \mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}_l, \mathcal{W}_l). \tag{3}$$

Recursively (xl+2 = xl+1 + F(xl+1, Wl+1) = x<sup>l</sup> + F(x<sup>l</sup> , Wl) + F(xl+1, Wl+1), etc.) we will have:

<span id="page-2-0"></span>
$$\mathbf{x}_{L} = \mathbf{x}_{l} + \sum_{i=l}^{L-1} \mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}_{i}, \mathcal{W}_{i}), \tag{4}$$

for any deeper unit L and any shallower unit l. Eqn.[\(4\)](#page-2-0) exhibits some nice properties. (i) The feature x<sup>L</sup> of any deeper unit L can be represented as the feature x<sup>l</sup> of any shallower unit l plus a residual function in a form of PL−<sup>1</sup> <sup>i</sup>=<sup>l</sup> F, indicating that the model is in a residual fashion between any units L and l. (ii) The feature x<sup>L</sup> = x<sup>0</sup> + PL−<sup>1</sup> <sup>i</sup>=0 F(x<sup>i</sup> , Wi), of any deep unit L, is the summation of the outputs of all preceding residual functions (plus x0). This is in contrast to a "plain network" where a feature <sup>Q</sup> <sup>x</sup><sup>L</sup> is a series of matrix-vector products, say, L−1 <sup>i</sup>=0 Wix<sup>0</sup> (ignoring BN and ReLU).

Eqn.[\(4\)](#page-2-0) also leads to nice backward propagation properties. Denoting the loss function as E, from the chain rule of backpropagation [\[9\]](#page-14-8) we have:

<span id="page-2-1"></span>
$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{E}}{\partial \mathbf{x}_{l}} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{E}}{\partial \mathbf{x}_{L}} \frac{\partial \mathbf{x}_{L}}{\partial \mathbf{x}_{l}} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{E}}{\partial \mathbf{x}_{L}} \left( 1 + \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathbf{x}_{l}} \sum_{i=l}^{L-1} \mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}_{i}, \mathcal{W}_{i}) \right).$$
 (5)

Eqn.[\(5\)](#page-2-1) indicates that the gradient <sup>∂</sup><sup>E</sup> ∂x<sup>l</sup> can be decomposed into two additive terms: a term of <sup>∂</sup><sup>E</sup> ∂x<sup>L</sup> that propagates information directly without concerning any weight layers, and another term of <sup>∂</sup><sup>E</sup> ∂xL ∂x<sup>l</sup> PL−<sup>1</sup> <sup>i</sup>=<sup>l</sup> F that propagates through the weight layers. The additive term of <sup>∂</sup><sup>E</sup> ∂x<sup>L</sup> ensures that information is directly propagated back to any shallower unit l. Eqn.[\(5\)](#page-2-1) also suggests that it

### Discussions

Eqn.[\(4\)](#page-2-0) and Eqn.[\(5\)](#page-2-1) suggest that the signal can be directly propagated from any unit to another, both forward and backward. The foundation of Eqn.[\(4\)](#page-2-0) is two identity mappings: (i) the identity skip connection h(xl) = x<sup>l</sup> , and (ii) the condition that f is an identity mapping.

These directly propagated information flows are represented by the grey arrows in Fig. [1,](#page-1-0) [2,](#page-4-0) and [4.](#page-7-1) And the above two conditions are true when these grey arrows cover no operations (expect addition) and thus are "clean". In the following two sections we separately investigate the impacts of the two conditions.

# <span id="page-3-0"></span>3 On the Importance of Identity Skip Connections

Let's consider a simple modification, h(xl) = λlx<sup>l</sup> , to break the identity shortcut:

$$\mathbf{x}_{l+1} = \lambda_l \mathbf{x}_l + \mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}_l, \mathcal{W}_l), \tag{6}$$

where λ<sup>l</sup> is a modulating scalar (for simplicity we still assume f is identity). Recursively applying this formulation we obtain an equation similar to Eqn. [\(4\)](#page-2-0): x<sup>L</sup> = (QL−<sup>1</sup> i=l λi)x<sup>l</sup> + PL−<sup>1</sup> i=l ( QL−<sup>1</sup> <sup>j</sup>=i+1 λ<sup>j</sup> )F(x<sup>i</sup> , Wi), or simply:

$$\mathbf{x}_{L} = \left(\prod_{i=l}^{L-1} \lambda_{i}\right) \mathbf{x}_{l} + \sum_{i=l}^{L-1} \hat{\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{x}_{i}, \mathcal{W}_{i}), \tag{7}$$

where the notation Fˆ absorbs the scalars into the residual functions. Similar to Eqn.[\(5\)](#page-2-1), we have backpropagation of the following form:

<span id="page-3-1"></span>
$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{E}}{\partial \mathbf{x}_{l}} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{E}}{\partial \mathbf{x}_{L}} \left( \left( \prod_{i=l}^{L-1} \lambda_{i} \right) + \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathbf{x}_{l}} \sum_{i=l}^{L-1} \hat{\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{x}_{i}, \mathcal{W}_{i}) \right). \tag{8}$$

Unlike Eqn.[\(5\)](#page-2-1), in Eqn.[\(8\)](#page-3-1) the first additive term is modulated by a factor Q<sup>L</sup>−<sup>1</sup> i=l λi . For an extremely deep network (L is large), if λ<sup>i</sup> > 1 for all i, this factor can be exponentially large; if λ<sup>i</sup> < 1 for all i, this factor can be exponentially small and vanish, which blocks the backpropagated signal from the shortcut and forces it to flow through the weight layers. This results in optimization difficulties as we show by experiments.

In the above analysis, the original identity skip connection in Eqn.[\(3\)](#page-2-5) is replaced with a simple scaling h(xl) = λlx<sup>l</sup> . If the skip connection h(xl) represents more complicated transforms (such as gating and 1×1 convolutions), in Eqn.[\(8\)](#page-3-1) the first term becomes Q<sup>L</sup>−<sup>1</sup> i=l h 0 <sup>i</sup> where h 0 is the derivative of h. This product may also impede information propagation and hamper the training procedure as witnessed in the following experiments.

This extremely deep ResNet-110 has 54 two-layer Residual Units (consisting of 3×3 convolutional layers) and is challenging for optimization. Our implementation details (see appendix) are the same as [\[1\]](#page-14-0). Throughout this paper we report the median accuracy of 5 runs for each architecture on CIFAR, reducing the impacts of random variations.

We investigate the "exclusive" gates as used in [\[6,](#page-14-5)[7\]](#page-14-6) — the F path is scaled by g(x) and the shortcut path is scaled by 1−g(x). See Fig [2\(](#page-4-0)c). The impact of the exclusive gating mechanism is two-fold. When 1 − g(x) approaches 1, the gated shortcut connections are closer to identity which helps information propagation; but in this case g(x) approaches 0 and suppresses the function F. Shortcut-only gating. In this case the function F is not scaled; only the shortcut path is gated by 1−g(x). See Fig [2\(](#page-4-0)d). The initialized value of b<sup>g</sup> is still essential in this case. This is also caused by higher training error (Fig [3\(](#page-6-0)c)).

#### 3.2 Discussions

As indicated by the grey arrows in Fig. [2,](#page-4-0) the shortcut connections are the most direct paths for the information to propagate. Multiplicative manipulations (scaling, gating, 1×1 convolutions, and dropout) on the shortcuts can hamper information propagation and lead to optimization problems.

It is noteworthy that the gating and 1×1 convolutional shortcuts introduce more parameters, and should have stronger representational abilities than identity shortcuts. In fact, the shortcut-only gating and 1×1 convolution cover the solution space of identity shortcuts (i.e., they could be optimized as identity shortcuts). However, their training error is higher than that of identity shortcuts, indicating that the degradation of these models is caused by optimization issues, instead of representational abilities.

### <span id="page-7-1"></span>4 On the Usage of Activation Functions

Experiments in the above section support the analysis in Eqn.[\(5\)](#page-2-1) and Eqn.[\(8\)](#page-3-1), both being derived under the assumption that the after-addition activation f

is the identity mapping. But in the above experiments f is ReLU as designed in [\[1\]](#page-14-0), so Eqn.[\(5\)](#page-2-1) and [\(8\)](#page-3-1) are approximate in the above experiments. Next we investigate the impact of f.

We want to make f an identity mapping, which is done by re-arranging the activation functions (ReLU and/or BN). The original Residual Unit in [\[1\]](#page-14-0) has a shape in Fig. [4\(](#page-7-0)a) — BN is used after each weight layer, and ReLU is adopted after BN except that the last ReLU in a Residual Unit is after elementwise addition (f = ReLU). Fig. [4\(](#page-7-0)b-e) show the alternatives we investigated, explained as following.

#### 4.1 Experiments on Activation

In this section we experiment with ResNet-110 and a 164-layer Bottleneck [\[1\]](#page-14-0) architecture (denoted as ResNet-164). A bottleneck Residual Unit consist of a 1×1 layer for reducing dimension, a 3×3 layer, and a 1×1 layer for restoring dimension. As designed in [\[1\]](#page-14-0), its computational complexity is similar to the two-3×3 Residual Unit. More details are in the appendix. The baseline ResNet-164 has a competitive result of 5.93% on CIFAR-10 (Table [2\)](#page-7-2).

BN after addition. Before turning f into an identity mapping, we go the opposite way by adopting BN after addition (Fig. [4\(](#page-7-0)b)). In this case f involves BN and ReLU. The results become considerably worse than the baseline (Table [2\)](#page-7-2). Unlike the original design, now the BN layer alters the signal that passes through the shortcut and impedes information propagation, as reflected by the difficulties on reducing training loss at the beginning of training (Fib. [6](#page-10-0) left).

ReLU before addition. A na¨ıve choice of making f into an identity mapping is to move the ReLU before addition (Fig. [4\(](#page-7-1)c)). However, this leads to a non-negative output from the transform F, while intuitively a "residual" function should take values in (−∞, +∞). As a result, the forward propagated signal is monotonically increasing. This may impact the representational ability, and the result is worse (7.84%, Table [2\)](#page-7-2) than the baseline. We expect to have a residual function taking values in (−∞, +∞). This condition is satisfied by other Residual Units including the following ones.

Post-activation or pre-activation? In the original design (Eqn.[\(1\)](#page-2-4) and Eqn.[\(2\)](#page-2-3)), the activation xl+1 = f(yl) affects both paths in the next Residual Unit: yl+1 = f(yl) + F(f(yl), Wl+1). Next we develop an asymmetric form where an activation ˆf only affects the F path: yl+1 = y<sup>l</sup> + F( ˆf(yl), Wl+1), for any l (Fig. [5](#page-9-0) (a) to (b)). By renaming the notations, we have the following form:

<span id="page-8-0"></span>
$$\mathbf{x}_{l+1} = \mathbf{x}_l + \mathcal{F}(\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_l), \mathcal{W}_l),. \tag{9}$$

It is easy to see that Eqn.[\(9\)](#page-8-0) is similar to Eqn.[\(4\)](#page-2-0), and can enable a backward formulation similar to Eqn.[\(5\)](#page-2-1). For this new Residual Unit as in Eqn.[\(9\)](#page-8-0), the new after-addition activation becomes an identity mapping. This design means that if a new after-addition activation ˆf is asymmetrically adopted, it is equivalent to recasting ˆf as the pre-activation of the next Residual Unit. This is illustrated in Fig. [5.](#page-9-0)

The distinction between post-activation/pre-activation is caused by the presence of the element-wise addition. For a plain network that has N layers, there are N − 1 activations (BN/ReLU), and it does not matter whether we think of them as post- or pre-activations. But for branched layers merged by addition, the position of activation matters.

We experiment with two such designs: (i) ReLU-only pre-activation (Fig. [4\(](#page-7-0)d)), and (ii) full pre-activation (Fig. [4\(](#page-7-0)e)) where BN and ReLU are both adopted before weight layers. Table [2](#page-7-2) shows that the ReLU-only pre-activation performs very similar to the baseline on ResNet-110/164. ### 4.2 Analysis

We find the impact of pre-activation is twofold. First, the optimization is further eased (comparing with the baseline ResNet) because f is an identity mapping. Second, using BN as pre-activation improves regularization of the models.

Ease of optimization. This effect is particularly obvious when training the 1001-layer ResNet. Fig. [1](#page-1-0) shows the curves. Using the original design in [\[1\]](#page-14-0), the training error is reduced very slowly at the beginning of training. For f = ReLU, the signal is impacted if it is negative, and when there are many Residual Units, this effect becomes prominent and Eqn.[\(3\)](#page-2-5) (so Eqn.[\(5\)](#page-2-1)) is not a good approximation. On the other hand, when f is an identity mapping, the signal can be propagated directly between any two units. Our 1001-layer network reduces the training loss very quickly (Fig. [1\)](#page-1-0). It also achieves the lowest loss among all models we investigated, suggesting the success of optimization.

We also find that the impact of f = ReLU is not severe when the ResNet has fewer layers (e.g., 164 in Fig. [6\(](#page-10-0)right)). The training curve seems to suffer a little bit at the beginning of training, but goes into a healthy status soon. By monitoring the responses we observe that this is because after some training, the weights are adjusted into a status such that y<sup>l</sup> in Eqn.[\(1\)](#page-2-4) is more frequently above zero and f does not truncate it (x<sup>l</sup> is always non-negative due to the previous ReLU, so y<sup>l</sup> is below zero only when the magnitude of F is very negative). The truncation, however, is more frequent when there are 1000 layers.

Reducing overfitting. Another impact of using the proposed pre-activation unit is on regularization, as shown in Fig. [6](#page-10-0) (right). The pre-activation version reaches slightly higher training loss at convergence, but produces lower test error. This phenomenon is observed on ResNet-110, ResNet-110(1-layer), and ResNet-164 on both CIFAR-10 and 100. This is presumably caused by BN's regularization effect [\[8\]](#page-14-7). In the original Residual Unit (Fig. [4\(](#page-7-0)a)), although the BN normalizes the signal, this is soon added to the shortcut and thus the merged signal is not normalized. This unnormalized signal is then used as the input of the next weight layer. On the contrary, in our pre-activation version, the inputs to all weight layers have been normalized.

# 6 Conclusions

This paper investigates the propagation formulations behind the connection mechanisms of deep residual networks. Our derivations imply that identity shortcut connections and identity after-addition activation are essential for making information propagation smooth. Ablation experiments demonstrate phenomena that are consistent with our derivations. We also present 1000-layer deep networks that can be easily trained and achieve improved accuracy.

Appendix: Implementation Details The implementation details and hyperparameters are the same as those in [\[1\]](#page-14-0). On CIFAR we use only the translation and flipping augmentation in [\[1\]](#page-14-0) for training. The learning rate starts from 0.1, and is divided by 10 at 32k and 48k iterations. Following [\[1\]](#page-14-0), for all CIFAR experiments we warm up the training by using a smaller learning rate of 0.01 at the beginning 400 iterations and go back to 0.1 after that, although we remark that this is not necessary for our proposed Residual Unit. The mini-batch size is 128 on 2 GPUs (64 each), the weight decay is 0.0001, the momentum is 0.9, and the weights are initialized as in [\[23\]](#page-14-22).

On ImageNet, we train the models using the same data augmentation as in [\[1\]](#page-14-0). The learning rate starts from 0.1 (no warming up), and is divided by 10 at 30 and 60 epochs. The mini-batch size is 256 on 8 GPUs (32 each). The weight decay, momentum, and weight initialization are the same as above.

When using the pre-activation Residual Units (Fig. [4\(](#page-7-0)d)(e) and Fig. [5\)](#page-9-0), we pay special attention to the first and the last Residual Units of the entire network. For the first Residual Unit (that follows a stand-alone convolutional layer, conv1), we adopt the first activation right after conv<sup>1</sup> and before splitting into two paths; for the last Residual Unit (followed by average pooling and a fullyconnected classifier), we adopt an extra activation right after its element-wise addition. These two special cases are the natural outcome when we obtain the pre-activation network via the modification procedure as shown in Fig. [5.](#page-9-0)

The bottleneck Residual Units (for ResNet-164/1001 on CIFAR) are constructed following [\[1\]](#page-14-0). For example, a " 3×3, 16 <sup>3</sup>×3, 16 # unit in ResNet-110 is replaced with a 1×1, 16 3×3, 16 1×1, 64 unit in ResNet-164, both of which have roughly the same number of parameters. For the bottleneck ResNets, when reducing the feature map size we use projection shortcuts [\[1\]](#page-14-0) for increasing dimensions, and when pre-

activation is used, these projection shortcuts are also with pre-activation.
